Threat-Aware Route Engine A terrain-aware, risk-sensitive 3D UAV mission planner — how it works, in detail.

A conventional planner finds the shortest path. This engine plans a complete survivable sortie — fly in, survive radar exposure by hiding in terrain, and get home on one tank of fuel — over real elevation data, and scores every candidate route.

real Copernicus DEM · any region terrain-placed threats ingress + egress sorties fuel-budgeted round trips radar-equation 1/R⁴ detection D* Lite incremental planner 15/15 tests

01The core idea

One vertical slice: data → algorithm → score → visualization. Terrain is one layer, radar visibility another; the planner fuses them into a single cost and searches for the route that best balances distance, threat exposure, fuel, terrain clearance, and time — for the whole round trip.

Shortest-path planner

Minimizes distance. Flies straight through radar coverage. Ignores that a low-flying aircraft's best defence is hiding behind terrain — and never checks if it can get home.

→

Survivability planner

Minimizes distance + threat exposure + energy + clearance, exploits terrain shadows, plans the return leg too, and rejects any sortie that runs out of fuel.

02The pipeline

Ten stages turn a heightmap and a threat list into a scored, flyable, visualized sortie.

1

Terrain / DEM

terrain.py · los.py

Elevation grid; height at any point by bilinear interpolation. Every mission reads a real Copernicus GLO-30 GeoTIFF through rasterio — Everest, Western Ghats, or any lat/lon.

Z[y,x] → ẑ(x,y)
2

Line-of-Sight · terrain masking

los.py

Sample the radar→aircraft line; if terrain rises above the sightline anywhere, the aircraft is masked (in the radar's shadow) and undetectable there.

3

Threat detectability field

risk_field.py

Each threat is a 3D detection volume; detectability follows the radar equation, gated by range, LOS, sector, cone, and minimum altitude, combined across radars. (Full detail in §03.)

4

3D planning grid + cost

planning_grid.py

Airspace → (x,y,altitude) nodes; below-terrain / low-clearance nodes are forbidden. Each edge carries a weighted cost of distance + exposure + climb energy + clearance.

5

Global planner · D* Lite

dstar_lite.py

Finds the minimum-cost route through the grid, for the ingress (base→target) and the egress (target→base) legs. Supports incremental re-planning when threats pop up.

6

Local refinement

smoothing.py · rrt_star.py

Risk-aware B-spline smoothing, or risk-aware RRT* with helicopter turn-rate and climb-rate limits for a genuinely flyable nap-of-the-earth trajectory.

7

Route metrics

route_metrics.py

Distance, flight time, energy/fuel proxy, integrated & peak exposure, min AGL clearance, climb/descent angles — per leg.

8

Survivability & mission scoring

scoring.py

Round-trip survivability, a fuel-budget feasibility check, and a full-sortie mission score. (Detail in §04–05.)

9

Monte-Carlo robustness

monte_carlo.py

Re-scores the ingress route ~50× under random aircraft-position and threat-location/strength jitter → a distribution, not a single fragile number.

10

Visualization

visualization.py · web_export.py · analysis.py

Four-panel dashboard, mission scorecard, Pareto & re-planning studies, and an interactive browser 3D export. (Every output explained in §08.)

03How threat detection works

A point in the air is "detected" by a radar only if it passes every gate below. This is why flying low in a valley beats flying straight.

1 · Range gate

Beyond the radar's maximum range R_max, detectability is exactly 0. Inside it, the signal still has to clear the remaining gates.

2 · Line-of-sight gate

If terrain blocks the straight line from radar to aircraft, detectability is 0 — the aircraft is in the radar's terrain shadow, regardless of range.

3 · Volume gate

Real emitters aren't perfect spheres: a horizontal sector, a vertical cone, and a minimum-altitude floor (e.g. a SAM that can't engage low targets) further restrict where a threat can see.

4 · Radar-equation strength

M(r) = intensity · 0.05 · (R_max / r)⁴

Received signal-to-noise scales as the inverse 4th power of range (the free-space radar equation). Normalized so detectability is 0.05 at max range and rises steeply toward the emitter.

Combining multiple radars

The probability that at least one radar detects you is the probabilistic union of the independent per-radar detectabilities:

R(x,y,z) = 1 − ∏i (1 − Ri) → always in [0, 1]

This R(x,y,z) field — the instantaneous detection risk at every point in the airspace — is what the planner's cost, the heatmap panel, and the survivability score are all built on.

04How the survivability score is built

Survivability is the probability the aircraft is never detected over the whole sortie. Here is exactly how the number (e.g. Western Ghats: 34% vs 69%) is produced.

Instantaneous risk along the path

At each point of the flown trajectory, evaluate the detection field R(x,y,z) from §03 — the chance of being seen right there, right now.

Integrate exposure over the route

Sum that risk along the path length (trapezoidal integration) to get cumulative exposure — how much "seen-ness" the whole trajectory accumulates.

Rint = ∫ R(x,y,z) · dℓ (over the flown path)

Convert exposure to a survival probability

Model detection as a Poisson process whose rate is proportional to exposure: the probability of zero detections over the sortie is the exponential of minus the integrated rate. This is the survival law the Stage-1 concept wrote as Psurvive = exp(−∫λdetect dt); we implement its normalized form with a tunable sensitivity λ = 0.05.

S = exp(−λ · Rint), λ = 0.05

Score the full sortie, not just one leg

A real mission is go and return, so exposure is summed over both the ingress and egress legs before applying the survival law:

Ssortie = exp(−λ · (Ringress + Regress))

Where the exp( ) comes from

The exponential isn't arbitrary — it falls out of one idea: to survive the whole flight you must survive every little piece of it, and you multiply those survival chances together. Cut the route into tiny segments; on each, the chance of being detected is small (local risk × segment length × sensitivity), so the chance of surviving it is just under 1:

survive segment i = 1 − λ·Ri·Δ

Surviving the whole route means surviving segment 1 and 2 and 3 … — independent events, so you multiply. Take the log (product → sum), use ln(1 − x) ≈ −x for small x, and the sum of the little risks is exactly the integrated exposure Rint:

S = ∏i(1 − λRiΔ) → ln S = Σ ln(1 − λRiΔ) ≈ −λ Σ RiΔ = −λ·Rint → S = exp(−λ·Rint)

So the exponential appears because survival compounds multiplicatively. It also keeps the answer sane — sliding smoothly from 1 (no exposure) toward 0 (huge exposure) but never past either, unlike a naive "1 − total risk" which could go negative. It's the same law behind radioactive decay, equipment reliability, and the Poisson "probability of zero events" (here the events are radar detections, expected number = λ·Rint).

See it in one number

A route of 3 segments, each a 10% chance of being spotted. Multiply the survivals directly: 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.729 (72.9%). Via the formula: each segment's risk is −ln(0.9) = 0.105, summed Rint = 0.315, and exp(−0.315) = 0.729 — the identical 72.9%. The formula is just the multiplication done all at once.

Worked example — Western Ghats (Anamalai)

The direct route flies over the auto-placed summit radar; the safe route detours through the valleys. Plugging the actual round-trip exposures into S = exp(−0.05·Rint):

Route profileRound-trip exposure RintSurvivability S = exp(−0.05·Rint)
Distance-focused21.72exp(−1.086) = 33.8%
Risk-focused7.41exp(−0.371) = 69.0%

Same terrain, same radars — the detour cuts exposure ~2.9× and lifts survival from 34% to 69%. That gap is the entire point of the engine.

05Full-sortie mission score & fuel budget

Survivability alone can pick a route you can't afford. The mission score folds in fuel and efficiency so the best number is a route that is survivable and makes it home.

Fuel budget

One fixed onboard budget, sized as a 15% reserve over the most fuel-efficient sortie found. The whole round trip (ingress + egress energy) must fit within it; a route that exceeds it is flagged FUEL-INFEASIBLE — it cannot get home.

Mission composite (0–100)

100·(0.5·S + 0.25·dist + 0.15·time + 0.10·clear) × fuel-factor

Round-trip survivability weighted highest, then efficiency and clearance. A fuel-infeasible sortie is halved (fuel-factor = 0.5), so it can't win the headline.

How the score ranks them — Western Ghats

RouteRound-tripSurvivability (I+E)FuelMission /100
Distance-focused341 m33.8%+13% OK46.1
Risk-focused425 m69.0%+4% OK58.6
Balanced425 m69.0%+4% OK58.6

Here all three sorties fit the fuel budget, so survivability decides: the risk-focused route wins (58.6) — 25% farther than the shortest, but ~2× more likely to come home. The shortest route scores worst precisely because it is the most exposed. The fuel budget is still the hard gate that would halve any "safe" route too long to make it home — the guard a single-leg planner never applies.

Threats are placed by the terrain, not by hand

A DEM is only elevation — it carries no radars. For every region the engine reads the terrain and the base→target corridor and auto-places the threat laydown (auto_place_hazards, risk_field.py), fully deterministic:

  • Search Radar (summit) — the tallest ridge/summit sitting on or near the direct corridor, so it commands the straight-line path and forces a detour.
  • Sector Radar (ridge) — a second dominant summit set well apart from the first, its coverage arc aimed at the corridor midpoint.
  • SAM (corridor) — high ground commanding the corridor midpoint (kept clear of the radars), with a minimum-engagement-altitude floor it is blind below.

Each emitter's detection range is auto-calibrated to the terrain: it is scaled until the direct base→target corridor carries a comparable threat exposure in every region, so the shortest path is always genuinely threatened and a survivable detour always exists — the survivability spread (the best-route signal) never collapses. The range geometry de-weights the exaggerated vertical of the normalized DEM, giving each radar a realistic horizontal footprint; divergence between routes then comes from leaving that footprint and hiding behind terrain.

…_pareto.png

Pareto risk-weight sensitivity study

Answers "how much should I weight safety?" It sweeps the risk weight w_risk across many values (0, 0.5, 1, 2, … 12) and re-plans a route for each. Two graphs:

  • Left — the Pareto front. Each dot is one w_risk setting; x = total distance, y = integrated exposure, colour = the weight. As the weight rises the dots move down-and-right: you buy safety with distance. The dashed line is the trade-off frontier; its "knee" is the best value.
  • Right — response curves. The same sweep as two lines vs w_risk: exposure (red, falling) and distance (cyan, rising) on twin axes. Shows the crossover and the point of diminishing returns (past ~6, more weight buys almost no extra safety).

Incremental re-planning demo

Injects a pop-up threat onto a flown route and repairs it with D* Lite. Shows the original vs re-planned route and the expansion-count comparison — the concrete justification for D* Lite over a one-shot planner (≈ 3.4× cheaper to repair than to re-plan from scratch).

…_3d.html

Interactive browser 3D

A self-contained web page you open in any browser. Unlike a fixed PNG, you can rotate, zoom, and pan the real terrain with the routes in true 3D, and toggle individual profiles / ingress / egress in the legend. It's how you actually verify a route hugs the valley, clears a ridge, or hides behind a summit — spatial relationships a flat image can't convey. Ideal for a live briefing.

09Algorithms — and what we don't use

Global · D* Lite

An incremental optimal graph search. Chosen because it repairs a plan cheaply when the map changes (a pop-up threat) instead of restarting — and it returns the true minimum-cost path.

Local · risk-aware RRT*

A sampling refiner with turn-rate and climb-rate limits, informed-tube sampling, and risk-aware shortcutting — turns the grid route into a flyable nap-of-the-earth trajectory.

Are you using RTA*? — No.

RTA* (Real-Time A*, Korf 1990) is a real-time agent search that commits to a move under a fixed lookahead, trading optimality for a bounded per-step deadline. This engine instead uses D* Lite, which computes the full optimal route and repairs it incrementally — the right fit for mission planning, where we want the best route and cheap replanning, not a per-step reflex.

We also do not use the reference paper's real-time MPC control layer; that online receding-horizon controller is noted as future work. Reinforcement learning is likewise out of scope for this prototype.
